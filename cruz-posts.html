Las Vegas winters are mild, but the roads leading out of town aren't always. Whether your crew is heading to a job in Utah, you're hauling a trailer to see family, or you're taking the RV somewhere for the holidays, cold temperatures and mountain grades can be tough on a vehicle that's used to desert driving.

A quick inspection before you leave can save you from a breakdown far from home.

1. Check your battery

Las Vegas summer heat weakens batteries, and the first cold snap is often what finishes them off. If your engine cranks slowly or your battery is more than three years old, have it tested before a long trip.

2. Inspect tires and tire pressure

Check tread depth and look for cracks, bulges, or uneven wear. Tire pressure drops as temperatures fall, so set it to the manufacturer's recommendation before you go. If you're towing, check the trailer tires too.

3. Look at coolant and other fluids

Make sure your coolant is at the right level and mixed for freezing temperatures if you're headed somewhere cold. Check engine oil, transmission fluid, brake fluid, and washer fluid as well.

4. Know your diesel's cold weather needs

Diesel engines can be harder to start in cold weather. Glow plugs, block heaters, and fuel filters all play a role. Diesel fuel can also thicken in very cold temperatures, so plan fuel stops and ask about winter blend fuel if you're traveling into freezing conditions.

5. Test brakes before mountain grades

Long downhill grades put extra heat on brakes, especially when you're loaded or towing. Squealing, grinding, a soft pedal, or pulling to one side are all signs to get your brakes checked before you leave.

6. Check lights, wipers and heater

Shorter days mean more driving in the dark. Make sure all lights work, wiper blades are in good shape, and your heater and defroster are working properly.

7. Pack an emergency kit

Bring water, snacks, blankets, gloves, a flashlight, jumper cables or a jump pack, and a phone charger. Check road conditions before heading over mountain passes.

No matter how well you maintain your fleet, breakdowns happen. A work truck won't start, a van overheats on the 215, or a shuttle bus throws a warning light in the middle of a shift. What matters most is how quickly you can get that vehicle running again.

Having a plan before it happens can save hours of downtime and a lot of stress.

1. Keep your shop's number handy

Make sure every driver knows who to call when something goes wrong. Save your repair shop's number in every driver's phone and keep it posted in each vehicle.

2. Teach drivers what to report

Ask drivers to note warning lights, unusual sounds, smells, leaks, and when the problem started. Photos of dashboard warning lights are especially helpful. The more information your shop gets up front, the faster they can diagnose the problem.

3. Know when to stop driving

Some problems can wait until the end of the day. Others can't. Overheating, a flashing check engine light, loss of oil pressure, brake problems, or smoke are all signs to pull over safely and stop driving. Pushing a vehicle with a serious problem can turn a repair into a replacement.

4. Have a towing plan

Know which towing company you'll use for each type of vehicle. Larger trucks, buses, and RVs often need heavy-duty towing, so it's worth knowing your options in advance.

5. Keep maintenance records organized

Service history helps technicians spot patterns and diagnose problems faster. Keep records for every vehicle in one place.

6. Plan for backup coverage

If you can, keep a spare vehicle available or have a plan to shift routes and jobs when one vehicle is down.

7. Use preventive maintenance to prevent the next one

The best downtime plan is fewer breakdowns. Regular inspections, fluid service, brake checks, and tire maintenance catch problems before they leave a vehicle on the side of the road.

Commercial vehicles are subject to inspection requirements, and a vehicle that fails can be taken out of service until repairs are made. For a business, that means lost time and lost revenue. The good news is that most inspection problems are preventable with routine maintenance.

Requirements depend on your vehicle type, weight, and how you operate, so check the regulations that apply to your fleet. Here are the areas inspectors commonly focus on.

Brakes

Brakes are one of the most common reasons vehicles fail inspection. Check pads, shoes, drums, rotors, hoses, and air brake components for wear, leaks, and proper adjustment.

Lights and reflectors

Every light needs to work, including headlights, brake lights, turn signals, clearance lights, and marker lights. Burned out bulbs and cracked lenses are easy to fix ahead of time.

Tires and wheels

Look for adequate tread depth, cuts, bulges, exposed cords, and proper inflation. Check wheels for cracks and missing or loose lug nuts.

Steering and suspension

Excessive play in the steering, worn components, and broken springs can all lead to a failed inspection and unsafe handling.

Leaks

Oil, coolant, fuel, and hydraulic leaks should be found and repaired before inspection.

Windshield, wipers and mirrors

Cracks in the driver's line of sight, worn wipers, and damaged mirrors are common issues that are simple to correct.

Exhaust and emissions

Exhaust leaks and emissions system problems can affect both safety and compliance, especially on diesel vehicles.

Daily driver inspections

Encourage drivers to complete a walk-around before and after each shift. Catching issues early makes inspections easier and keeps vehicles safer on the road.

The end of the year is a great time to look back at how your fleet performed and plan for the year ahead. A little planning now can help you control repair costs, reduce surprises, and keep vehicles on the road in 2027.

1. Review this year's maintenance and repair costs

Look at what you spent on each vehicle. Which ones cost the most? Were most of the costs routine maintenance or unexpected repairs? This tells you where your money is going.

2. Identify problem vehicles

Every fleet has a few vehicles that seem to spend more time in the shop than on the road. Compare repair costs, downtime, and mileage to decide whether to keep repairing them or plan a replacement.

3. Build a preventive maintenance schedule

Set service intervals for oil changes, inspections, brakes, tires, and fluid services based on mileage, hours, and how each vehicle is used. Scheduled maintenance costs far less than emergency repairs.

4. Plan for the Las Vegas summer

Extreme heat is hard on batteries, cooling systems, AC, and tires. Budget for pre-summer service in the spring so your vehicles are ready before temperatures climb.

5. Set aside a repair reserve

Even well-maintained vehicles need unexpected repairs. A repair reserve makes it easier to approve work quickly and keep vehicles moving.

6. Talk to your repair shop

Your shop sees your vehicles up close. Ask which vehicles are showing signs of wear, what repairs may be coming, and how to schedule service around your busy seasons.
